When the moon appears, I say goodnight to my wife and *laku noć* to my daughter. *Laku noć* means ‘goodnight’ in Bosnian, the second language that I’ve been teaching my daughter.

Bosnian is my first language, which I never formally learned. Every word that I read, write, and speak in Bosnian has been learned informally, in passing, through conversations with my family since childhood. My parents preserved our native language by strictly speaking it at home, forcing me to learn it and communicate with them.

Now I also plan to preserve the language by only speaking it with my daughter, with my insecurity in mind. Because I never formally learned to speak it due to the [Bosnian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnian_War), I wonder if I have sufficient fluency, which I define as the ability to complete a task despite incomplete knowledge, to teach my daughter to not only read, write, and speak in the language but do so correctly.

Over the years, my native vocabulary and syntax has improved enough to feel sufficiently comfortable in conversation. I’ve spoken at length with my people and walked away with understanding. I’ve written messages to family and friends over the years too and felt fine about how and what I’ve said.

But there have been uncomfortable times like when I’m speaking with my parents. I start telling them a story and as the sory develops, my vocabularly dwindles and my trust in words dissolves and my feeling from childhood echoes … The searching, the reaching for the exact word spirals into a temporary identity crisis, alleviated by a meandering description that helps, it seems, my parents nod in understanding.

It’s possible that this occasional identity crisis results from a culturally divorced upbringing. While we spoke Bosnian at home, I spoke English otherwise and constantly straddled the two worlds. I’ve always debated internally which road to take like in [that Robert Frost poem](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/44272/the-road-not-taken), all with the hopes that the fork ends up a spork.

As I’ve meditated on spoonfeeding my daughter native words, I’ve considered enrolling in language courses to improve my Bosnian. I learn quickly, but the informality of my previous learning feels part of me, leaving me to wonder if the shared learning experience with my daughter would be more beneficial than becoming a stickler for proper usage.

Yet I feel like a failure knowing that my daughter will learn from a half-savage fool. Imagine sending your child to school to learn a subject knowing that their teacher *kind of* knows what they are talking about—and you’re the teacher.

But maybe I’ve mistakened the goal. Maybe learning is not about acing the test. Maybe learning is about the lighting and preserving the eternal flame of curiosity. Maybe an incomplete but sufficient start leads to continued learning, later mastery, and correlative learning. My learning has led to deep research, whether it’s using Google Translate to improve my vocabulary, asking my parents questions about my ancestors, reading stories about historical battles, or learning about sub-cultures in the old country. That’s led to a dynamic understanding that one would not presume if they only judged by my mastery of the Bosnian language.

Maybe mastery is impossible, and curiosity is only possible. Curiosity is fundamental to learning, which is the building block to knowledge that begins with an imperfect first word in a second language.
