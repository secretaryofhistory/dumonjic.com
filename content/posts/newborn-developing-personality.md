One night, I held our daughter after her feeding and rocked her in place back and forth, back and forth, like the previous nights. She laid across my arms, her head held in the “L” of my left elbow, her side squeezed by my left forearm and hand, her back stabilized by my right arm. It was the perfect position to lull her asleep like the previous nights.

While her eyes were closed like before, the smile was replaced by a gaping mouth with a little tongue ringing like an alarm bell. I tried transforming into a human white-noise machine by gently shushing her. I rocked fast and faster, hoping that a quicker pace would soothe her. I tried raising her up with my lower arm, so she was lying higher up against my chest (it worked that one time). I tried to control my breathing by taking a deep breath to inspire her (it also worked once). I tried patting her butt. I tried running my index finger up and down the center of her forehead, which usually calms her.

I started to pace the space between her crib and our bed like it were a courtyard. I walked six steps parallel to the beds, completed a one-step turn, and repeated it down the opposite side, forming a rectangular pathway. Suddenly our daughter opened her mouth wider than before, and yawned. She took a deep breath. I laid her into the crib and then straightened up, wondering what happened to the rocking routine we had formed days earlier …

I realized that our newborn—a person with preferences apparently—no longer liked the rocking; now she liked the pacing.

A night later, she liked the pacing without stepping on the round green rug, it seemed, because the slight elevation caused her to open her eyes and furrow her brow multiple times; confirmed the next night.

Here was a newbie, I thought, changing policy without congressional approval. Here was a one-week-old changing overnight, and that phrase parents say over and over about kids growing up fast was now a reality. I saw it happen. I wondered how it happened.

In the days since, I still wondered about the change because our daughter literally couldn’t speak to the difference between rocking and pacing.

In the simplest and generalized understanding, I imagine she feels the movement and decides if she likes it. If so, she remembers it and looks for it when the time comes again. If not, she cries until she feels comfortable like an adult tossing and turning in bed till discovering the soft spot in the mattress before finally falling asleep.

I also imagine that the preference between rocking and pacing is of predictability. Rocking is more predictable, stable, and consistent of the two. It’s a seesaw-like motion, inherently predictable due to the limited possibilities, which may bore our daughter. It’s like sleeplessly staring at the ceiling, hoping it does *something*.

If our daughter’s brain is like mine or my wife’s, it finds the rocking dull compared to pacing. Pacing is inherently unpredictable despite the six steps and one-step turn because strides change in length and the motion of our bodies shift. As a result, our bodies exert varying levels of energy with each step, causing them to become more tired and sleepier. At least that’s what I think our perceptive daughter felt when she cried for her preference.

I wonder what she thinks.
