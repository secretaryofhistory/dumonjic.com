Last Saturday afternoon, I was watching our daughter and found myself with nothing to say, which was unusual.

Usually, she plays with her toys, and I narrate made-up stories in Bosnian, like her stuffed friend Mickey Mouse has made a surprise visit to her house, wants to give her a hug and a kiss, asks how her day is going, and so on. The story develops in real-time the longer I speak, making me feel like Homer or a village bard, and our daughter generally enjoys it until she doesn’t, which means she’s become hungry.

But this time was different. Mickey was mute. I did not have the mental energy to tell a story, and regardless of how many times I tried, my mind could not form a sentence. It felt like my mind was a lawnmower and I kept pulling the cord, but it would not start.

I felt like a failure in some ways, as if I couldn’t entertain my daughter by simply speaking with her, and at one point briefly laid on the play mat, wondering what the problem and solution were. After all, it was a long workweek and a sleepless night, and I ate a lot of pasta salad.

It also felt like a disservice to my daughter, who likes to be engaged, likes to be read books, to track toys as they move around the room, to reply in conversation (grunts, groans, etc.) and smile.

In the moment, I finally realized that I had more physical energy than mental energy and pivoted to an outdoor walk, so I put our daughter in the stroller and off we went for an hour down toward Main Street, which was enjoyable walk because a summer breeze carried us past beautiful houses and landscaping typical of New England, lulling our daughter to sleep for the hour.

I felt relieved during the walk because I’d found a solution for the exhausted moments. The walk provided my mind a break from active engagement, healing it with passive engagement, and it provided time for our daughter to nap in plain air.
