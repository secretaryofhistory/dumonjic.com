And out she went, legs first. Then she laid on the table, encircled by four registered nurses (RNs) who wiped her clean and patted her until the sudden cry; and the room, relieved, released a deep breath …

After thirty-nine weeks, our daughter officially brought much-anticipated light to this [pale blue dot](https://m.youtube.com/watch?v=GO5FwsblpT8&list=RDGO5FwsblpT8&start_radio=1&pp=ygUYcGFsZSBibHVlIGRvdCBjYXJsIHNhZ2FuoAcB). As did her mother, my wife, a superhuman carrying our family in her stomach, supplying our family life’s essentials every day, willing every inch of life until it could sustain itself.

Before the birth, I stood in room S215A alone, staring out the window at the flurries twirling from the sky, sizing up the future:

I would become the sun to a planet in minutes, yet it felt the inverse at the same time. I would be teaching our daughter manners, cultures, languages. I would be introducing her (selectively) to so many people. How would I introduce her? By affiliation? By name? Isn’t it inappropriate to tell a lady’s age?

I was also fighting intrusive thoughts; the usual worst-case scenarios that everyone feared hearing, seeing, processing. I fought and fought them until Molly, the lighthearted RN, came into the room, laughing.

—You’re … you’re wearing the shoe scrub on your head!

We laughed together before I fixed it and followed her into the delivery room.

In the room, I held my wife’s hand and waited for that moment of liveliness, listening to the surgeon and her staff trading tools, cutting, clanking. I looked down at the floor, hoping to stare at nothingness and calm my breathing like a monk, when I saw blood, pooling. I looked at my wife, who was lying on her back and staring with her ocean-colored pupils into mine.

—See anything? She said.

An RN from behind reached forward between us:

—It’s about time. Lowering the drape, she said.

The blue drape lowered and now a clear one hung. The surgeon said something. A leg rose magically like a ballerina’s. Another leg. A rear. She was coming out the wrong way and, for some reason, I remembered when [Joey stuck his head in the turkey](https://m.youtube.com/watch?v=Q1JwgIsQb5M&pp=ygUbam9leSB0cmliYmlhbmkgdGhhbmtzZ2l2aW5n) in the one Thanksgiving episode, and suddenly we had life. I looked over at my wife:

Her eyes were watery.

I looked over to Molly and three RNs with whom our daughter lay. After technical difficulties with the oxygen monitor that felt like eons of troubleshooting, and after using the inherent talent that only nurses and mothers possess, they assured us that our daughter was perfect.
