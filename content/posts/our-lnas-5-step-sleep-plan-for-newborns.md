At the Women and Children’s Health Unit (WACHU) in our local hospital, the nurses told stories about a Licensed Nursing Assistant (LNA) who lulled babies to sleep easily.

In one story, she swaddled a newborn and lulled them to sleep. Shortly after, a nurse said a health check was needed, proceeded to undo the swaddle, completed the check which caused the newborn to cry, and then the LNA re-swaddled the newborn and lulled her to sleep again in minutes.

So when the LNA started coming around our newborn daughter, I watched closely to learn from the legend. It was five steps that I still complete every night:

1.  Swaddle high and tight
2.  Hold them chest-high with both arms
3.  Place a white noise maker on them
4.  Rock in place
5.  Rub their forehead

Starting with the swaddle, I continued using the [hospital’s newborn blankets](https://a.co/d/0eC17Oh0) because I preferred their starchiness and thickness to the modern stretchy ones.

Picture the rectangular blanket on a bed end to end with the pattern facing the mattress, leaving the white inside facing you. With the top of the blanket folded an inch or so down for a soft edge (varies with baby), I laid our daughter atop the blanket with her shoulders aligned with the fold, creating a continuous horizontal line.

Next, I rested one of our daughter’s arms down the length of her body and diagonally stretched the blanket from that same side across the shoulder, the arm, the chest, and the stomach, and tucked the blanket behind the back, near her opposite hip. It created a diagonal line from the near shoulder to the far hip. Then I repeated the steps for the opposite side.

The final part to the swaddle was the bottom half’s loose ends. The goal was to make the bottom half as high and tight as the top half, so the finished swaddle looks like a chick rising out of an egg like the emoji (🐣). To achieve this, I flared out each end of the bottom half, shaping it like a mermaid’s tail, to create a straight line across and then pulled them up to our daughter’s chin. This flipped the bottom half to the top, tucking our daughter’s legs as if she were in a potato sack. Then I slightly raised our daughter, wrapped one end around the back, and repeated that on the other side to complete the swaddle.

After completing the swaddle, I held our daughter high to my chest with the back of her head resting in the L-shape of my elbow, my forearm down her side, and the opposite arm supporting the butt and back. Soon I learned two things that our daughter liked:

-   She liked when I was completely calm, so I would take a deep breath to empty my lungs upon first holding her
-   She liked to have her butt patted while she was trying to fall asleep, so my lower palm rested on the bottom-half of her diaper and my upper palm gently patted her top-half

Another thing that I learned was the secret shush of the white-noise maker. The thing hurts my ears, but the LNA said it works on all babies, which is true of ours too. We bought the same [Skip Hop owl](https://a.co/d/0c4Nu4RO) that the hospital had (and later bought 5 more that we donated to the hospital because they had only 1). I placed it directly on our daughter’s swaddled abdomen when holding her at night and moved onto the next step: pacing.

Our LNA did not pace; she stood in place, rocking side to side. I tried that, but my hips don’t move and our daughter noticed it too. I switched to pacing around the room inside, which [our daughter much preferred](/2026/03/15/newborn-developing-personality/).

The goal of the steps thus far was to see the moment when our daughter drew a deep breath and relaxed her body, indicating that she was finally asleep.

After seeing the deep breath, I slowly stopped patting our daughter’s butt and kept my palm on it to see if she woke up.

If she remained asleep, I switched from patting her butt to lightly running my index finger or thumb up her forehead. Starting halfway down the bridge of her nose, I gently moved the side of my fingertip up the center of her forehead to her hairline. Then I lifted my finger and repeated the process. This lasted a couple of minutes and caused the pacifier to fall out of our her mouth.

The culmination of this was not that her eyes closed and she could be laid in her crib, but that the pacifier falling out revealed our daughter’s smile. It’s the warmest, lovelist smile that I have ever seen. It’s the smile that, when I lied down in bed to close my eyes, drew a deep breath and a smile from me too.
