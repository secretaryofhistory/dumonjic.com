Every day, my wife and I put our daughter in the car and drive aimlessly for ~2 hours. The primary reason for the drive is to sneak in another nap for our daughter, who sleeps the entire ride most of the time, and the secondary reason is for us to have a mental break.

Our mental break can consist of silence during the ride, which lets us enjoy the woodsy and hilly scenery surrounding us, or of conversation. The conversation is often a check-in of how we think our daughter is progressing, how we’re doing today mentally and physically, and how it feels to be a parent. The answer to the last one has been steadily refined over the last six weeks and recently defined as *unique but human*.

By “unique,” I mean that it’s unlike any other experience that I’ve ever had. I can’t think of anything else that has such severe and lifelong consequences for leading a careless everyday life. Every word uttered and step taken must be curated, requiring a complete slowdown which is more challenging than expected because of how much our collective consciousness lives on autopilot.

As someone who spends a lot of time thinking about how my mind works, I’ve learned some actions occur beyond my purview, inspired by genetic traits or assumptions developed by my mind. My mind voluntarily infers from its surroundings and makes decisions for me; the same decisionmaking applies based on prior knowledge of the surroundings and of my preferences. All of this seems shaped over time by micro-interactions that formed micro-personalities within my mind and body. It’s like I’m a baked good that tastes slightly different based on the baker’s influence over the recipe.

The challenge with this is my mind’s autopilot can mislead my parenting and misshape our daughter’s personality, so I really try to keep my hands on the wheel at all times by assessing every situation uniquely without influence, assumption, or prepackaged solution. I try to understand every situation from the root or fundamental problem, reason my way to a solution, and apply it.

For example, our daughter recently returned overstimulated from a hip checkup. She spent one hour at the doctor’s office, and it really overwhelmed her because she has interacted with very few people for very short stretches in these first 60 days while her immune system builds. Naturally, she cried and cried from the overstimulation. Instinctually, I consoled her by hugs, kisses, songs, etc. That was the worst thing that I could’ve done because she needed less interaction and sensory stimulation. While she cried, I troubleshooted potential solutions (confirmed it’s possible) and decided on a bath. She sat in warm water for 10 minutes as my wife gently cleaned her with a cloth. Then she ate and fell asleep for one of her longest sleeps yet.

I previously reserved that type of root-cause problem-solving for the workplace, which had little consequence compared to molding a human being with healthy skills and traits needed to survive a lifetime. That’s the unique part of parenting.

The other part of parenting, which I referred to as “human,” is that our daughter is simply the same species as us, giving us a head start. She’s made from the same fabrics of the world as I am, including my DNA, making it easier to understand and raise her. This part is simultaneously a potential trap because familiar situations can breed autopilot thinking, so I really try to think thoughtfully and avoid assumptions with our daughter.

Acknowledging this human part is key, I think, because it grounds the entire parenting experience in sobering but required reality. It removes the gilding that leads to parents thinking their kids are special, when the kids are actually like everyone else and parenting is unlike anything else.
