It happened at two weeks and five days, two days before the pediatrician’s recommendation. Our newborn was lying in bed after a multi-breast feed, munching on air as if she were still hungry despite her oval belly expanding well beyond her hips.

—I know it’s a couple of days early, said my wife, but what do you think about giving her the pacifier?

I nodded. Our newborn had developed a habit of lingering on her mother after finishing breastfeeding, sucking the soul out of her mother.

Having previously boiled the pacifier(s) for the meeting of newborn and false nipple, my wife opened the closet, took one pacifier out of the sanitized container, and gently introduced it. Our newborn took to it immediately, impatient and indifferent.

Then it fell out. We quickly returned it.

Then it fell out again …

As the days passed, these events repeated, resulting in multiple pacifiers becoming unusable because they fell out onto the floor and needed disinfecting (pacifier clips order delayed). Thankfully, her mother was always prepared with another clean one.

With the last clean one, we kept close watch on its whereabouts, including how close it was to falling out of our daughter’s mouth, how near we were to a clean landing spot in case it fell beyond us, and what the chances were of us pushing it back into our daughter’s mouth by leaning toward her with our chest when she was rocking in our arms. If not, how would we survive the whole 5 minutes that it took to boil and disinfect?

Days later, during a routine diaper change of which I was in charge, the pacifier started to fall out and to save it I used the quickest thing nearby, our daughter’s hand. I moved her right arm back, which is always raised like a dictator’s (or flailing because she was breeched), and placed her hand on the pacifier, and she held it in!

Over the next couple of diaper changes, I again assisted her with holding in the pacifier. She held it in each time despite it sliding around her mouth or seemingly slipping underneath her tongue, which temporarily upset her.

Then one night I was changing her diaper and, from the corner of my left eye, spying the pacifier slipping out again. I prepared myself by holding the diaper up (stops sprinklers) with my right hand and reaching out with my left hand to push in the pacifier, when our daughter raised her hand and pushed in the pacifier and resumed sucking it.

I stopped. I watched proudly, like all parents of their children’s most minute achievements. Here she was only a few weeks old, learning a new skill after a couple of demonstrations, after recognizing repeatable patterns. Suddenly a world of learning possibilities opened up.
