A friend asked what was the hardest part of surviving a newborn. Like many, he guessed sleeplessness. I shook my head side to side because I’ve always slept little. Then he guessed other commonly noticed symptoms, but I continued shaking my head.

—[Hypervigilance](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/hypervigilance), I said, interrupting him.

Hypervigilance has been the hardest adjustment in early fatherhood. It’s an involuntary change since our daughter’s first cry that has taken my strength—hyper focus, often a productivity hack—and repurposed it for hyper awareness to ensure I always watched over our daughter.

My mind never stops listening for specific sounds, my eyes never stop looking for our daughter, my nose never even stops smelling for dirty diapers. Trying to fall asleep in the wee hours? Working downstairs midday? My mind’s there, anytime, anywhere.

The part particularly challenging is the crying because we own a townhome that shares walls with other babies. Believe it or not, other babies cry too, and at times I listen to the cries carefully to decode the neighbor’s from our daughter’s. Sometimes I’m so unsure that I climb halfway up the stairs and listen even more carefully before making a determination.

One time I couldn’t figure it out. I went all the way upstairs, approached our bedroom door with my ear, and discovered that it was not our daughter crying; not this time, anyway. By the time I descended the stairs, I recognized the familiar cry of our daughter.

Another time I sat downstairs by myself, working. The window was cracked open with a brisk breeze sneaking through, clearing the warmth trapped overnight. It felt meditative, so I drew a deep breath, when I sniffed a funk. A dirty diaper! I looked around, but our daughter was not there. It was impossible to smell a dirty diaper one floor down; hot air rises. Was I hallucinating?

Since then, I’ve wondered whether this evolutionary mental step into fatherhood is a double-edged sword. It seems productive at times, but *all the time*? My mind never rests, resulting in more exhaustion than usual, causing me to make mistakes and forget things in other areas of my life.

It’s also led me to wonder if placing kids in daycare is a benefit to dual working parents. I imagine that parents potentially don’t have that same sensory overload when their mind *knows* that their kid is not home. Yet it’s possible that parents also have mental overload in other ways, like always wondering whether their kid is safe and cared for at daycare.

It seems that parents, whether first-timers or veterans, all experience this same evolutionary step, and it’s to the benefit to the safety and wellness of the kids.
